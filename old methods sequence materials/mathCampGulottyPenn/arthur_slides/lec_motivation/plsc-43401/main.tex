\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Why Do We Need to Learn Calculus}

\ \\Example 1: Derivative in Positive Political Economy \pause

\ \\We consider an example on redistributive distortionary taxation. The example comes from a textbook, Political Economics: Explaining Economic Policy, by Persson and Tabellini. \pause

\ \\The individual has quasi-linear preferences:
$$w = c + V(x)$$
where $c$ and $x$ denote individual consumption and leisure, and $V()$ is a well-behaved concave utility function. \pause

\ \\The private budget constraint is:
$$c \leq (1 - \tau)l + f$$
where $\tau$ denotes the income tax rate, $l$ the individual labor supply, and $f$ a lump sum transfer.

\end{frame}



\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Why Do We Need to Learn Calculus}

\ \\Example 1: Derivative in Positive Political Economy

\ \\In addition to that, individuals are subject to the "time constraint":
$$\alpha \geq x + l$$
where $\alpha$ is individual productivity. \pause

\ \\We can maximize individual's utility subject to the budget and time constraints. \pause

\ \\The first-oder condition implies:
$$1 - \tau = \frac{\partial V } {\partial x}(\alpha - l)$$
where a subscript denotes a derivative. \pause

\ \\Then, we can get individual's labor supply: $l = \alpha - \frac{\partial V } {\partial x}^{-1}(1 - \tau)$.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Why Do We Need to Learn Calculus}

\ \\Example 2: Integral in Statistics \pause

\ \\Suppose that you get a very decent score, say, 1500. \pause

\ \\A natural question to ask is: what's the probability of getting a score higher than 1500 in SAT test in 2018.

\ \\We need to compute this probability by: \pause

$$Pr(x \geq 1500) = \int_{1500}^{+ \infty} {\frac {1}{\sqrt {2\pi \sigma ^{2}}}}e^{-{\frac {(x-\mu )^{2}}{2\sigma ^{2}}}} dx = 0.04779$$
where $\sigma = 300$, $\mu = 1000$.
